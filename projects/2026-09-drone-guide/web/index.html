How a drone flies and how to build your own

A quadcopter has no wings and no steering wheel. It has four spinning propellers, a tiny computer for a brain, and one trick: it changes how fast each propeller spins. This guide shows you exactly how that trick works, and then how to build a drone that does it.

Part 1

How it works

1 · Meet the quadcopter

Eight parts, one flying robot

Quad means four. Copter comes from helicopter. So a quadcopter is a helicopter with four rotors. Here it is, pulled apart so you can see inside. Tap any part.

Tap a part to learn what it does

Each part has one job. Together they make a machine that can hang in the air.

2 · Going up

Push air down, go up

Blow up a balloon and let it go. Air rushes out one way, and the balloon zooms the other way. Every push makes an equal push back. Scientists call this Newton's third law.

A drone uses the same idea. Its propellers shove air down, so the air shoves the drone up. That upward push is called thrust. At the same time, gravity pulls the drone down. Whichever is stronger wins.

Gravity wins. The drone sits on the ground.

  1. At 0%, gravity wins. The drone sits.
  2. At 50%, thrust equals gravity. The drone hangs in the air. That's a hover.
  3. Above 50%, thrust wins. It climbs.
Big idea: at full power this drone pushes twice its own weight. Builders aim for at least that, so there is always spare power for steering and fighting wind.

3 · The propeller's secret

Why a spinning blade pushes air

Riding in a car with a grown-up, hold your flat hand out the window. Now tip the front edge up a little. The wind lifts your hand. That tilt is the whole secret of a propeller.

Each blade is a small tilted wing. As it spins, its front edge slices in and its tilted face throws air downward. A flat blade just slices through the air and pushes nothing.

Try it

The drone lifts off.

Big idea: propellers are named by size and tilt. A 5×4 prop is 5 inches across. The 4 means that if it were a screw turning into wood, one full turn would move it 4 inches forward. More tilt pushes more air per turn but needs a stronger motor.

4 · The twist problem

Why two spin one way and two the other

Here's a puzzle. When a motor spins its propeller one way, the propeller twists the motor, and the whole drone, the other way.

Test it at home: sit on a swivel chair with your feet off the floor. Swing both arms hard to the left. The chair turns you to the right.

So drones use two clockwise propellers and two counter-clockwise propellers, set on opposite corners. Their twists cancel out, and the drone keeps pointing the same way.

Try it

Two twist one way, two twist the other. They cancel. The drone holds still.

Big idea: helicopters have the same problem. That's what the little tail rotor is for: it pushes sideways so the body doesn't spin.

5 · Steering

Steering with nothing but speed

A drone has no steering wheel and no flaps. It moves only by spinning some motors faster than others. Hold a button and watch the motor map in the corner.

Pitch nod your head "yes": tip forward or back.
Roll tip your head to a shoulder: lean left or right.
Yaw shake your head "no": turn in place.

Hold a button.

Big idea: a drone always pushes straight out of its top. To go somewhere, it tips its top that way, and part of the push now points where it wants to go.

6 · The brain

The brain that keeps it level

Balance a broom upright on your hand. A tiny bump and it starts to tip, and nothing pulls it back. A drone is just as wobbly. So how does it hover so still?

The flight controller catches every wobble, the same way you catch the broom:

  1. Feel. A tiny sensor called a gyroscope feels which way the drone is tipping, and how fast.
  2. Compare. The brain compares that with how it should be: level.
  3. Fix. It speeds up the motors on the low side to push that side back up.

Then it does it again. Many drone brains run this loop 4,000 times every second.

Big idea: turn the brain off and send a gust. With no one catching it, the tip keeps going, and the drone falls.

7 · Inside the wires

Follow the power

Two things flow through a drone: energy that spins the motors, and messages that say how fast. Step through to follow them.

  1. Battery. Energy starts here, stored as chemicals waiting to push electricity out.
  2. Speed controllers. They take the battery's energy and chop it into very fast pulses for each motor. More pulses, faster spin.
  3. Motors. Inside each motor, a ring of wire coils surrounds a ring of magnets. The spinner below shows the trick.
  4. Receiver. Messages start at your controller. The receiver catches them by radio.
  5. Flight controller. The brain mixes your commands with what its sensor feels, then sends each speed controller one number: how fast.

How an electric motor spins. The speed controller switches the coils on one after another around the circle. Each lit coil becomes a magnet for a moment and pulls the spinning part toward it. Round and round, like a stadium wave the spinner keeps chasing.

8 · Take the controls

Fly it yourself

A drone controller has two sticks. This is the most common layout, called Mode 2:

  • Left stick: up and down to climb or sink, left and right to turn.
  • Right stick: pushes the drone the way you point it: forward, back, left, right.

Try it

Use the two sticks on the 3D view above. Drag a stick and let go: it springs back to the middle, just like a real one.

Keyboard: W S A D for the left stick, arrow keys for the right.

Pilot tip: keep the red lights (the back) facing you. If the drone turns around, its left becomes your right, and even experienced pilots get mixed up.

Build your own

9 · Where to start

Three levels of drone builder

Level 1

Learn to fly

Practice on a drone simulator on a computer, then fly a small toy drone with prop guards. Crashes cost nothing, and every skill carries over.

Level 2

Build a kit

A kit with plug-in parts. You put together the frame, motors, board, and propellers, then set it up on a computer. Little or no soldering.

Level 3

Design your own

Choose every part yourself, solder the wires with a grown-up, and tune it with free software. Racing and camera drones are built this way.

Start at Level 1, even if you can't wait to build. Learning to fly on a drone you built means crashing a drone you built.

10 · Parts and tools

What you need

Every quadcopter, from a toy to a racer, is made of the same eight parts you met in Part 1. Plus a few tools:

  • Small hex screwdrivers
  • Zip ties and double-sided foam tape
  • A radio controller
  • A battery charger made for LiPo batteries
  • A computer for setup
  • A soldering iron grown-up

Safety rules

  • Props off whenever you work on the drone. Spinning blades cut.
  • LiPo batteries hold a lot of energy. Charge only with a grown-up nearby, on a fireproof surface. A puffy, swollen battery is finished: stop using it.
  • Soldering irons are hotter than any oven. Grown-ups solder; everyone wears safety glasses.

12 · Setup

Test before you fly

Before the props go on, plug the drone into a computer and open the free setup app. The most common one is called Betaflight. Tilt the drone in your hands and you'll see the model on screen tilt too: that's the gyroscope working.

  • Bind: pair your controller with the receiver so they only listen to each other.
  • Arm switch: pick a switch that turns the motors on. Motors off is called disarmed: safe.
  • Failsafe: if the radio signal is lost, the motors should stop, so the drone can't fly away.
  • Motor check: spin each motor one at a time and make sure it turns the right way.

14 · Flying outside

The rules of the sky

In the United States, the FAA (Federal Aviation Administration) sets the rules for flying for fun:

  • Take the free safety test called TRUST before you fly outside. Kids can take it; there's no minimum age.
  • Keep it in sight. You must always be able to see your drone with your own eyes.
  • Stay low: at or below 400 feet.
  • Big drones get registered. A drone of 250 grams or more needs an FAA number. You must be 13 to register, so a grown-up registers it for you.

Tiny drones flown indoors are the easiest place to start. Before flying somewhere new outside, a grown-up can check the FAA's B4UFLY app to see where drones are allowed.

Drone words

Thrust
The push from the propellers.
Hover
Thrust equals gravity, so the drone hangs still.
Throttle
How much power goes to all four motors.
Pitch
Tipping nose down or up.
Roll
Tipping to the left or right.
Yaw
Turning in place.
Gyroscope
The sensor that feels tipping and turning.
ESC
Electronic speed controller: feeds power to one motor.
LiPo
Lithium polymer: the light, powerful battery drones use.
FPV
First-person view: seeing through the drone's camera.
Arm
Switch the motors on. Disarm to switch them off.
Failsafe
What the drone does if it loses the radio signal.
