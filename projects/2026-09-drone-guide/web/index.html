How a drone flies and how you'll build your own

Welcome to the workshop, {pilot}.

A quadcopter has no wings and no steering wheel. It has four spinning propellers, a tiny computer for a brain, and one trick: it changes how fast each propeller spins.

Eight parts, one flying robot

Quad means four. Copter comes from helicopter. So a quadcopter is a helicopter with four rotors.

Let's meet its parts one at a time, {pilot}.

The frame

The skeleton. Four carbon-fiber arms hold the motors far apart. Carbon fiber is stiff, light, and very hard to break.

Green lights mark the front. Red lights mark the back.

The motors

Inside each motor is a ring of copper coils. Around them spins a bell full of magnets.

The coils switch on one after another, and the magnets chase them around. That's what makes it spin.

The propellers

Each blade is twisted and tilted, like a little wing. Spinning them throws air down, and that pushes the drone up.

The battery

The fuel tank. A LiPo (lithium polymer) battery packs a lot of energy into a small, light pack.

The strap holds it tight. The yellow plug carries its power to the drone.

The speed controllers

Four electronic speed controllers (ESC for short) share one board. Each feeds one motor exactly as much power as the brain asks for.

The flight controller

The brain. A motion sensor on this board feels every tilt, and the chip decides how fast each motor should spin.

The colored arrows show what the sensor feels as the drone rocks.

The radio receiver

The ears. It listens for radio messages from your controller and passes each one to the brain.

The two antennas point up and back, away from the propellers.

The camera

An FPV (first-person view) camera sends live video to goggles, so you see what the drone sees.

All together

Eight parts, each with one job. Tap any label to meet that part again.

Push air down, go up

Blow up a balloon and let it go. Air rushes out one way, and the balloon zooms the other way.

Every push makes an equal push back. That's Newton's third law.

Thrust vs. gravity

Propellers push air down, so the air pushes the drone up. That push is thrust. Gravity pulls down. The stronger one wins.

Half power: a hover

At 50% power, thrust equals gravity, so the drone hangs in the air.

Big idea: At full power this drone pushes twice its own weight. Builders aim for at least that, so there's spare power to steer and fight wind.

Why a spinning blade pushes air

Riding in a car with a grown-up, hold your flat hand out the window. Tip the front edge up a little. The wind lifts your hand.

That tilt is the whole secret of a propeller.

Tilt the blades

A tilted blade scoops air and throws it down. A flat blade just slices through.

Try it

The drone lifts off.

Reading a prop's name

Props are named by size and tilt. A 5×4 prop is 5 inches across.

The 4 means: if it were a screw turning into wood, one full turn would move it 4 inches forward.

The twist problem

When a motor spins its propeller one way, the propeller twists the whole drone the other way.

Test it at home: Sit on a swivel chair with your feet up. Swing both arms hard to the left. The chair turns you to the right.

Two one way, two the other

So drones use two clockwise and two counter-clockwise props on opposite corners. Their twists cancel out.

Try it

Two twist one way, two the other. They cancel. The drone holds still.

Helicopters have it too

That's what a helicopter's little tail rotor is for: it pushes sideways so the body doesn't spin.

Steering with nothing but speed

A drone has no steering wheel and no flaps. It moves only by spinning some motors faster than others.

Three ways to tip

Pitch Nod "yes": tip forward or back.
Roll Tip your head to a shoulder: lean left or right.
Yaw Shake "no": turn in place.

Tip the push

Big idea: A drone always pushes straight out of its top. To go somewhere, it tips its top that way, and part of the push points where it wants to go.

The brain that keeps it level

Balance a broom upright on your hand. A tiny bump and it starts to tip. A drone is just as wobbly.

Feel, compare, fix

  1. Feel. A gyroscope sensor feels which way it's tipping.
  2. Compare. The brain checks that against level.
  3. Fix. It speeds up the motors on the low side.

Many drone brains run this loop 4,000 times every second.

Follow the power

Two things flow through a drone: energy that spins the motors and messages that say how fast.

Energy starts in the battery, stored as chemicals.

Speed controllers

They chop the battery's energy into very fast pulses for each motor. More pulses, faster spin.

Motors

The pulses switch the copper coils on one after another. The magnets chase them around, like a stadium wave.

Receiver

Messages start at your controller. The receiver catches them by radio.

Flight controller

The brain mixes your commands with what its sensor feels, then sends each speed controller one number: how fast.

Your turn, {pilot}

Left stick: up and down to climb or sink, sideways to turn. Right stick: push the way you want to go.

Keyboard: W S A D, and the arrow keys.

Pilot tip

Keep the red lights (the back) facing you. If the drone turns around, its left becomes your right, and even experienced pilots get mixed up.

Level 1

Learn to fly

Every pilot starts here, {pilot} included. Practice on a drone simulator on a computer, then fly a small toy drone with prop guards. Crashes cost nothing, and every skill carries over.

Level 2

Build a kit

A kit with plug-in parts. You put together the frame, motors, board and propellers, then set it up on a computer. Little or no soldering.

Level 3

Design your own

Choose every part yourself, solder the wires with a grown-up, and tune it with free software. Racing and camera drones are built this way.

Tip: Start at Level 1, even if you can't wait to build. Learning to fly on a drone you built means crashing a drone you built.

What you need

The eight parts you met in Part 1, plus:

  • Small hex screwdrivers
  • Zip ties and foam tape
  • A radio controller
  • A LiPo battery charger
  • A computer for setup
  • A soldering iron grown-up

Safety rules

  • Props off whenever you work on the drone. Spinning blades cut.
  • LiPo batteries hold a lot of energy. Charge only with a grown-up nearby, on a fireproof surface. A puffy battery is finished.
  • Soldering irons are hotter than any oven. Grown-ups solder; everyone wears safety glasses.

Test before you fly

With the props off, plug the drone into a computer and open the free setup app. The most common one is Betaflight.

Tilt the drone in your hands and the model on screen tilts too: that's the gyroscope working.

Three settings

  • Bind: pair your controller and receiver so they only listen to each other.
  • Arm switch: a switch that turns the motors on. Off is disarmed: safe.
  • Failsafe: if the radio signal is lost, the motors stop.

The rules of the sky

In the United States, the FAA (Federal Aviation Administration) sets the rules for flying for fun:

  • Take the free TRUST safety test before flying outside. Kids can take it.
  • Keep it in sight at all times.
  • Stay at or below 400 feet.
  • Register drones of 250 grams or more. You must be 13, so a grown-up registers it.
